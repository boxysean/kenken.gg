About kenken.gg

How photo scanning works, explained like you're five

You take a photo of a KenKen. The computer has to turn that photo into a puzzle it can solve. It can't look at the whole thing at once the way you do, so it works in small steps, one after another, like following a recipe.

If any step goes wrong, everything after it goes wrong too: if the corners are off, the cages and clues will be too. That's why each step gets checked on its own (see the report card below).

Step 4 is the hard one: teaching the computer to read

Steps 1–3 follow fixed rules. Reading a clue is different: there are many fonts, and newspapers, books and printouts all write 2− or 12÷ a little differently. So instead of writing rules, we teach a small program, the reader, the way a child learns letters: with flash cards. Each card shows a picture of a character, with the right answer on the back.

+ → →

Under the hood: the reader is a small convolutional neural network (CNN), about 90,000 parameters. It's a classifier with 14 classes: the digits 0–9 and + − × ÷. The flash cards are labelled training data and studying is supervised learning: the network is trained in PyTorch with the Adam optimiser on a cross-entropy loss. The practice cards are synthetic data, made more varied by data augmentation (random rotation, noise and brightness). The finished network is exported to ONNX and runs in your browser with onnxruntime-web (WebAssembly). If the model can't load, the app falls back to simpler template matching.

The reader only knows what its flash cards showed it. One trained only on neat practice cards gets confused by a real newspaper's font, much as you might be the first time you saw cursive. Real cards fix that, and those come from labelled photos.

Under the hood: this is called domain shift, or the sim-to-real gap. The reader scores 98.6% validation accuracy on synthetic crops, but it has never seen a real font, so it doesn't generalise to them. The fixes are more varied synthetic data (more fonts) and fine-tuning on real examples.

Labelling a photo: making the answer key

A labelled photo is a photo plus an answer key: what a person says is really in it. Anyone can make one in the Label tab:

→ →

One answer key is useful twice. It's a test: run the photo through the five steps and compare each one with the key. And it's a source of real flash cards: every clue in the photo becomes a card with its answer already known.

Under the hood: the answer key is the ground truth. In machine-learning terms, labelling is data annotation, and the exported JSON holds the four corner coordinates, the cage map and every clue. Before an answer key is trusted, a solver checks that its puzzle has exactly one solution. A labelling mistake almost always breaks that.

The report card that only goes up

Every labelled photo is kept, and every change to the scanner is graded against all of them, one step at a time. The rule is simple: a grade can go up, but it can never go down. A change that improves one photo but breaks another isn't allowed in.

Under the hood: this is a regression test run in continuous integration (CI) on every change, with a ratchet: the scores are saved as a baseline that only moves up. Each step is graded on its own by feeding it the ground truth from the steps before it, so a failure upstream can't hide a failure downstream (an oracle, or ablation, evaluation).

Privacy notice

  • kenken.gg runs entirely in your browser. Puzzles you enter and photos you scan or label are never sent to a server.
  • Your Play progress and theme choice are saved in your browser's local storage, on your device only.
  • The kenken.gg website uses GoatCounter to collect web browsing information.
